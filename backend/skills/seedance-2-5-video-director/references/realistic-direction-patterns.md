## Control depth

Match prompt depth to the user's intent and source material.

- **Compact:** one subject, one beat, usually under 10 seconds. Use intent, action, camera, sound, and essential locks.
- **Sequenced:** a 10-30 second scene with several state changes. Use global setup plus readable chronological beats.
- **High-control:** use when the user asks for detail, supplies a layered example, needs exact performance/physics, or requires live multicamera grammar. Use asset locks, global world, timed execution, performance or physics, audio, camera rules, and final invariants.

Do not mistake brevity for stability. Reduce conflicting or impossible instructions first; preserve detail that controls identity, causality, timing, camera purpose, or emotional state.

## Example-transfer contract

When a user supplies an example, separate these dimensions:

1. hierarchy and section order;
2. detail density and timestamp granularity;
3. story events and ending;
4. camera POV, shot family, and cutting grammar;
5. performance logic and emotional arc;
6. visual style, era, light, and texture;
7. asset-reference scope;
8. dialogue, sound, and subtitle policy.

Transfer only explicit dimensions. `参考这个结构`, `按这个结构写`, or `只参考提示词结构` means inherit hierarchy, control depth, and comparable granularity only. Do not inherit first-person POV, one-take form, wardrobe, plot, dialogue, props, setting, or ending unless requested.

When revising, propagate every story change into the timeline, physics/performance section, sound, camera rules, continuity, and final mandatory sequence. Remove stale constraints from the old version.

## Natural human performance

Direct a motivated reaction chain:

`stimulus → comprehension pause → first involuntary response → chosen response → residual emotion → next beat`

- Put the character's objective, fear, defense, and contradiction before microgestures.
- Use 1-3 visible cues per beat: gaze shift, breath catch, incomplete gesture, grip change, posture change, swallowed line, or delayed reply.
- Let the previous emotion remain during the next beat. A smile fades, laughter decelerates, anger leaks into hurt, and resolve can coexist with affection.
- Allow natural asymmetry, blinking, small focus drift, self-correction, interrupted gestures, and conversational overlap.
- Describe anatomy only when it is visually necessary. Avoid sequential checklists for eyebrows, eyelids, nostrils, cheeks, and mouth; they produce mechanical acting.

For conflicted emotion, show two impulses through behavior. Examples: a hand loosens from a suitcase handle and grips it again; a character almost steps closer, then restores distance; lips part to agree, then the gaze falls and the answer changes.

### Blush, tears, and laughter

- Treat blush as optional. If needed, make it slight, gradual, uneven, and physiologically motivated. Prefer gaze avoidance, altered breath, posture, and speech rhythm over sudden uniform red cheeks.
- Accumulate tears before release: breath changes, eyes moisten, blink timing changes, then one tear may cross the lid at the specified late beat. Do not start with full tears unless the scene starts after prior crying.
- Build laughter from suppression or a trigger, then preserve residual breath and shoulder movement. Do not jump from neutral to exaggerated laughter and back to neutral.
- Preserve real skin texture; prohibit beauty-filter blush, plastic skin, and emoji-like expression switching when realism matters.

## Dialogue and emotional conflict

Budget dialogue for performance, not reading speed. As a rough Mandarin heuristic, allow about 3-5 Chinese characters per second for natural emotional delivery; a brief argument can reach 5-6, while hesitation, crying, or subtext needs more time. Count pauses, listening, interruption, breath, and action inside the interval.

Build short conflict scenes around:

1. each character's immediate objective;
2. the surface issue;
3. the defense or misunderstanding;
4. the deeper reveal;
5. the listening reaction;
6. a choice or changed relationship state.

Keep dialogue conversational: fragments, self-corrections, incomplete sentences, and repeated everyday phrasing are often more credible than polished slogans. Give the listener screen time. The decisive emotional beat may be the reaction after a line, not the line itself.

For a breakup or reconciliation, avoid writing one character as emotionally finished unless requested. Show attachment and resolve together: instinctively defending the partner, reacting to a familiar promise, nearly yielding, then choosing based on accumulated history.

## Relationship-drama camera grammar

First obey explicit form: POV versus third person, one take versus edited coverage, same-frame permissions, shot-scale limits, and aspect ratio.

For conventional third-person drama:

- establish location, prop, distance, and the active relationship state with a wide or medium-wide shot;
- use clean singles or shot/reverse-shot as requested;
- maintain the 180-degree axis, screen direction, eyeline height, light direction, and background geography;
- cut for a reason: speaker emphasis, listener comprehension, a withheld reaction, a prop action, or a changed power balance;
- enter closer framings as emotional pressure increases, but do not fill the frame with a face when prohibited;
- hold after important lines so the reaction can develop before the next cut;
- use J- or L-cuts only when they preserve spatial clarity and conversational flow.

If `不要两人同框`, exclude shoulders, silhouettes, reflections, mirrors, foreground blur, and accidental background appearance of the other principal. An establishing shot can contain only one character plus environment unless the user explicitly permits both.

For one-take scenes, timestamp headings are performance beats, not cuts. Change framing only through plausible camera or subject movement; do not use cut language, viewpoint jumps, or impossible lens changes.

Do not infer POV or handheld style from an example described as a structural reference.

## Sports and variety live coverage

Treat live coverage as action explanation, not generic cinematic motion.

1. Establish course geography, travel direction, obstacle order, finish state, and camera placement.
2. Assign each camera a purpose: crane/wide for geography, long lens for anticipation, side track for locomotion, low/water camera for scale and contact, ENG medium for recovery or reaction, finish camera for resolution.
3. Cut at action nodes while preserving progress, screen direction, body state, obstacle phase, and the subject's wetness/fatigue.
4. Show enough of the obstacle before impact so the viewer understands why contact occurs.
5. Make commentary, crowd, mechanism sounds, music, and graphics react after the visible event.

Use a high-control layered structure when requested:

`global live style → complete character lock → course continuity → exact timeline → physical feedback → performance arc → sound design → camera rules → final mandatory sequence`

Do not add unplaceable orbiting cameras, cinematic teleportation, repeated actions, or a replay unless requested. Use fictional, minimal broadcast graphics; avoid real network branding and unreliable dense text.

## Physical causality

For obstacles, falls, collisions, water, props, clothing, and other consequential action, write:

`cause or mechanism → approach → contact → force direction → body/object response → recovery or outcome → next state`

- No body reaction before contact.
- No fall, splash, button activation, light, or sound before its trigger.
- Preserve gravity, inertia, surface traction, object rotation, pendulum phase, water displacement, and contact shadows at the level visible in the shot.
- Keep wardrobe, hair, wetness, fatigue, damage, held props, and location changes cumulative.
- Reduce event count if the full causal chain cannot fit the interval.

## Contradiction audit

Resolve these before drafting:

- casual phone video versus extreme shallow depth of field or impossible stabilized motion;
- fixed social distance versus face-filling close-up;
- one take versus cut, reverse-shot, or instant viewpoint change;
- third person versus camera owned by a character;
- no two-shot versus shoulders, reflections, or silhouettes of both characters;
- full-character reference versus redesigned wardrobe or accessories;
- exact duration versus dialogue and actions that cannot fit;
- natural acting versus muscle-by-muscle expression checklists;
- no BGM versus requested score;
- protected source footage versus a global restyle or edit;
- changed story outcome versus stale physics, sound, camera, or final constraints from the old outcome.

When a lower-priority style wish conflicts with an explicit camera, identity, duration, or edit lock, preserve the lock.
